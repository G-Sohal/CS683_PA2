\section{Data Prefetching}
\subsection{Understanding IP stride code}
The IP-stride prefetcher tracks memory accesses separately for each instruction pointer (IP). It stores the previous cache block address, observed stride, and LRU information.

For each access, it calculates the stride between the current and previous block addresses. If consecutive strides match, it predicts future addresses and issues prefetch requests for up to three blocks.

The prefetcher avoids crossing 4 KB page boundaries and selects L1 or L2 as the prefetch destination based on MSHR occupancy. When the tracking table is full, it replaces the least-recently-used entry.

Thus, it exploits regular memory access patterns to fetch data in advance, while irregular or changing strides can reduce its effectiveness.

    The RPT records the cache lines prefetched recently. It is used both as a duplicate filter (a line that is already in the RPT is not prefetched again) and by the accuracy checker described below.

    \item \textbf{Threshold for Accuracy Throttling:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{What:} Accuracy is evaluated over epochs of 256 prefetches (\texttt{EPOCH\_PREFETCHES}). A prefetch is counted once it fills the L1D (or when a demand load catches it in flight), and it is counted as useful when a demand load hits the prefetched line in the RPT. At the end of each epoch:
        \begin{equation}
            \text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{useful prefetches in epoch}}{256} \times 100\%
        \end{equation}
        Both counters are then reset. Three static thresholds divide accuracy into four regions:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{High Accuracy ($\ge 75\%$):} at least three out of four prefetches are used.
            \item \textbf{Medium Accuracy ($50\% \le \text{Accuracy} < 75\%$):} most prefetches are used.
            \item \textbf{Low Accuracy ($25\% \le \text{Accuracy} < 50\%$):} at most half of the prefetches are used; a higher risk of displacing useful lines.
            \item \textbf{Poor Accuracy ($< 25\%$):} fewer than one in four prefetches is used; prefetching mostly causes pollution and queue contention.
        \end{itemize}

        \item \textbf{Why:} An epoch of 256 prefetches follows program phase changes quickly while averaging out short bursts. The thresholds are evenly spaced quarters, which keeps the hardware comparison trivial (the useful count compared against 64, 128, and 192) and balances memory latency hiding against cache pollution:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Above 75\%, prefetching rarely evicts useful demand lines, which justifies the deepest lookahead.
            \item Between 50\% and 75\%, moderate prefetching gives good coverage without congesting the L1D MSHRs.
            \item Below 25\%, more than three quarters of the prefetched lines are never used, so the degree is reduced to the minimum.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

    The prefetcher starts at degree 2 and moves to the degree of the measured accuracy range at the end of every epoch.

    \textbf{Rationale for Selected Degrees:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Degree 4 ($\ge 75\%$):} When accuracy is high, chaining lookahead through 4 consecutive strides in the Pattern Table (e.g., $(+1, +2, +2, +2)$) pulls data far in advance, overlapping the L1D miss latency (15 cycles to the L2) without stalling the execution core.
        \item \textbf{Degree 3 ($50\% \le \text{Accuracy} < 75\%$):} Serves as the balanced operating point (matching the degree of IP-stride). It keeps prefetch bandwidth high while staying comfortably within the L1D prefetch queue capacity (16 entries).
        \item \textbf{Degree 2 ($25\% \le \text{Accuracy} < 50\%$):} Reduces speculative lookahead depth when stride transitions become irregular. Fetching only 2 blocks bounds cache-line displacement while still prefetching one line further ahead than the minimum.
        \item \textbf{Degree 1 ($< 25\%$):} Acts as a protective lower bound. Rather than turning prefetching off entirely (which would stop the accuracy checker from observing the prefetcher and recovering when the program enters a new phase), Degree 1 only issues the immediate next predicted line until accuracy improves.
    \end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rationale Behind Insertion Policy}
In standard Static RRIP (SRRIP), all newly filled blocks are inserted with an intermediate prediction value $RRPV = 2$ ($M-1$ in an $M$-bit counter where $M=2$, $\max RRPV = 3$). In I-RRIP, we differentiate between instruction lines and data lines upon insertion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instruction Lines:} Assigned $RRPV = 1$. By granting instruction lines a lower initial RRPV than data lines, they are placed closer to the MRU state ($RRPV = 0$) and require multiple aging increments before becoming eviction candidates. This provides retention priority, directly protecting instructions from premature eviction.
    \item \textbf{Data Lines:} Assigned $RRPV = 2$. Data lines follow standard distant re-reference prediction, ensuring they do not crowd out instruction lines unless they exhibit repeated temporal reuse.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rationale Behind Promotion Policy}
Upon a cache hit, both instruction and data lines are promoted directly to $RRPV = 0$ (MRU). Preserving standard hit promotion to 0 guarantees that active working set lines that repeatedly hit remain protected regardless of their type, maintaining high hit rates for frequently accessed data while preventing unnecessary cache thrashing.

\subsubsection{Rationale Behind Eviction Policy}
Standard RRIP chooses the first line with $RRPV = 3$ and increments all lines if none is at $\max RRPV$. In I-RRIP, the eviction policy is instruction-conscious:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary Candidate Search:} The victim search first targets non-instruction lines that have reached $RRPV = \max RRPV$. If a non-instruction line is found at $RRPV = 3$, it is immediately evicted.
    \item \textbf{Fallback Candidate Search:} If all blocks in the set with $RRPV = 3$ are instruction lines, the policy checks whether \emph{any} non-instruction lines exist in the set. If non-instruction lines exist (even if their RRPV is currently $< 3$), the policy ages all lines (increments RRPV) rather than evicting the instruction line, thereby driving the non-instruction lines toward $RRPV = 3$. Only if a set consists strictly of instruction lines is an instruction line evicted at $RRPV = 3$.
\end{itemize}
This prioritization dramatically cuts instruction misses: on Trace 2, Instruction MPKI drops from 19.53 to 9.28 (a 52.5\% reduction), raising IPC from 0.2506 to 0.2603 (+3.85\% speedup).

\subsubsection{Rationale for the Number of Leader Sets and Decision Logic}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Number of Leader Sets:} ChampSim's L2 cache contains 1024 sets. We designate 32 leader sets distributed uniformly using modular indexing ($\text{set} \pmod{32} == 0$), which corresponds to exactly $32/1024 = 3.125\%$ (or 1 in 32 sets) of the cache. As demonstrated in classic set-dueling literature (Qureshi et al., ISCA 2006), 32 sampled sets provide statistically significant representation of total workload cache behavior across diverse access patterns while bounding the penalty of suboptimal leader sets to less than $3.2\%$.
    \item \textbf{Dedicated Leader Set Operation:} The 32 leader sets unconditionally run standard RRIP. They track four hardware counters: $\text{leader\_instr\_access}$, $\text{leader\_instr\_miss}$, $\text{leader\_total\_access}$, and $\text{leader\_total\_miss}$.
    \item \textbf{Decision Formula:} The follower sets query the sampled instruction intensity ratio:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{leader\_instr\_access} + \text{leader\_instr\_miss}}{\text{leader\_total\_access} + \text{leader\_total\_miss}}
    \end{equation}
    If $\text{Ratio} > \theta$, the follower sets operate in I-RRIP mode (prioritizing instruction lines in insertion and eviction). When $\text{Ratio} \le \theta$, follower sets fall back to standard RRIP mode.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Phase Tracking and Aging:} To allow the policy to adapt rapidly to application phase changes and prevent counter saturation, all four leader counters are halved (right-shifted by 1) whenever $\text{leader\_total\_access}$ reaches an epoch of 4096 accesses. This decaying window prevents stale execution history from dominating future replacement decisions.
    \item \textbf{Threshold Sensitivity Analysis:}
    As seen in Table~\ref{tab:dyn-irrip-threshold} and Figures~\ref{fig:dyn-irrip-perf-sensitivity} and~\ref{fig:dyn-irrip-mpki-sensitivity}, lower thresholds ($\theta = 0.15, 0.20$) allow follower sets to activate I-RRIP aggressively, yielding maximum instruction miss reduction (Trace 2 Instr MPKI drops from 18.55 down to 9.54, giving 1.0357$\times$ speedup). As the threshold is increased to 0.35, the policy becomes more conservative, falling back to RRIP more often, which slightly raises instruction MPKI but curtails data miss increases (on Trace 1, Data MPKI drops from 9.91 to 9.22). Threshold $\theta = 0.25$ provides an ideal balance across workloads.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Rationale Behind Prefetch-Aware Replacement Policy}
The Dynamic I-RRIP policy of Section~\ref{sec:dyn-irrip} ($\theta = 0.25$, 32 leader sets) is extended with four rules that apply only to requests whose packet type is \texttt{PREFETCH}. Without a data prefetcher no such requests reach the L2, so the policy then behaves exactly as in Task 2B; we verified that the Dynamic I-RRIP rows of Table~\ref{tab:task3-performance} are identical to the $\theta = 0.25$ results of Table~\ref{tab:dyn-irrip-performance}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insertion:} Demand lines keep the Task 2B insertion (instruction lines at $RRPV = 1$ and data lines at $RRPV = 2$ in I-RRIP mode, all lines at $RRPV = 2$ in RRIP mode). A line filled by a prefetch is inserted at $RRPV = 2$ and marked with a per-line \emph{prefetched} bit. Because SPP fills the L1D with the same block, the L2 copy is only a backup that matters if the prefetch is useful, so it is never given instruction-level priority.
    \item \textbf{Promotion:} A \texttt{PREFETCH} request that hits in the L2 does not promote the line, because a prefetch is not evidence of reuse. A demand hit on a prefetched line promotes it to $RRPV = 0$ and clears the prefetched bit; the prefetch was useful and the line is treated as ordinary data from then on.
    \item \textbf{Eviction:} Before the Task 2B victim search, the policy evicts an unused prefetched line that has reached $RRPV = 3$, so inaccurate prefetches leave the cache before any demand data or instruction line.
    \item \textbf{Set dueling:} \texttt{PREFETCH} requests are not counted in the leader-set counters. Otherwise prefetch traffic would lower the measured instruction ratio and could switch the follower sets out of I-RRIP mode even though instruction pressure is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Results and discussion:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The combination beats both components on every trace} (Table~\ref{tab:task3-performance}). SPP and Dynamic I-RRIP remove different misses: SPP lowers L1D and L2 data misses, while Dynamic I-RRIP lowers L2 instruction misses. On \textbf{Trace 1} the combined IPC is \textbf{0.2379} ($1.0243\times$) against 0.2339 for SPP and 0.2356 for Dynamic I-RRIP. On \textbf{Trace 2}, where instruction misses dominate, the combination keeps both benefits: L2 instruction MPKI falls from 19.53 to 9.72 (the Dynamic I-RRIP effect) and L1D MPKI falls from 13.54 to 13.22 (the SPP effect), giving an IPC of \textbf{0.2610} ($1.0412\times$). On \textbf{Trace 3}, which is data-bound, most of the gain comes from SPP, and the combination reaches \textbf{0.3888} ($1.0179\times$).
    \item \textbf{SPP accuracy and coverage are essentially unchanged} by the L2 policy (within about 1 percentage point), because the prefetcher trains on L1D accesses and its usefulness is measured at the L1D.
    \item \textbf{Prefetch awareness has a small effect} (Table~\ref{tab:task3-pf-aware}). The final policy lowers L2 instruction MPKI on Trace 2 (9.85 to 9.72) and Trace 3 (1.00 to 0.98), but speedups stay within 0.05\% of the unmodified policy. The reason is the low prefetch volume reaching the L2: \texttt{PREFETCH} requests are only 7.9\%, 1.6\%, and 15.1\% of L2 read requests on Traces 1, 2, and 3, so there is little prefetch pollution for the policy to remove.
    \item \textbf{Choice of $RRPV_{pf} = 2$:} Inserting prefetches at $RRPV = 3$ protects instructions slightly more on Trace 2 (L2 instruction MPKI 9.68 vs 9.72) but is slower on Traces 1 and 3. The two settings are within 0.15\% on every trace, and $RRPV = 2$ gives the higher mean speedup ($1.0278\times$ vs $1.0276\times$), so it is used as the final configuration.
\end{itemize}
